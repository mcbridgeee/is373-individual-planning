**Dr. Thorne:** Good to see you again, Jenny. How has the past week been since we last met?

**Jenny:** Honestly, pretty rough. I haven't had another full panic episode at the office, which is a relief, I guess, but I am completely exhausted. My brain just refuses to shut down at night.

**Dr. Thorne:** Tell me more about what your nights have looked like this week.

**Jenny:** I'll get into bed around eleven, totally wiped out from staring at spreadsheets all day, and then the second my head hits the pillow, my mind starts racing. Most nights this week I was wide awake, up until 3 a.m. just staring at the ceiling or scrolling on my phone because lying still felt unbearable. **Dr. Thorne:** Being up until 3 a.m. night after night takes a massive toll on your nervous system's baseline. When your body is running on empty like that, your threshold for anxiety drops dramatically during the day.

**Jenny:** That makes sense, because yesterday afternoon my chest started getting that tight, floaty feeling again when my boss walked past my cubicle. **Dr. Thorne:** Since you're experiencing that physical escalation both at your desk and when you're lying awake in the middle of the night, I want to introduce our first concrete coping strategy today. It's a paced breathing exercise called physiological sighing—or box breathing, depending on which feels more natural to you. Let's try a simple 4-4-6 pattern right now.

**Jenny:** Okay. How does that work?

**Dr. Thorne:** You inhale slowly through your nose for a count of four, hold gently at the top for four, and then exhale slowly through your mouth for a count of six. Making the exhale longer than the inhale sends a direct physical signal through your vagus nerve to slow your heart rate down. Let's do three rounds together right here so you can feel how it lands.

**Jenny:** *(Follows along for three cycles, drops shoulders slightly on the third exhale)* Okay, yeah. That actually made my shoulders drop a little bit. It feels weird to slow down that much when my brain wants to sprint.

**Dr. Thorne:** That's completely normal at first. This week, whenever you feel that chest tightness at work, or when you notice you're lying awake past midnight, I want you to practice four or five rounds of that 4-4-6 breathing exercise. Don't expect it to magically fix everything overnight—just treat it as a way to hit the brakes when your body starts accelerating.

**Jenny:** Alright, I can do that. Anything to stop staring at my ceiling until three in the morning.
